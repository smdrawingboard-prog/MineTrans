Before you renew

Four reasons a schedule pays out short.

Each one can be checked against your own paperwork in an afternoon.

01

The figure is last year's

A sum insured carried forward from last year's schedule is the most common reason a machinery claim settles short. Plant is bought, sold and moved between sites faster than the schedule is revisited.

02

Being wrong is not neutral. It is proportional.

Under-declaring the value can trigger average at claims stage, reducing the settlement in proportion to the shortfall. The difference is not absorbed by the insurer. It is carried by the operator, at the worst possible moment.

03

Replacement is not book value

Cover should reflect what it costs to replace with equivalent equipment delivered and commissioned at your site, not a book or trade-in figure. For imported plant that means exchange rate movement, freight and duties, which move independently of the machine.

04

The policy may not respond at all

All-risk answers sudden external damage. Machinery breakdown answers internal failure. A machine that fails internally with no external damage is a breakdown claim, so a programme carrying only all-risk leaves that exposure open.

Basis of valuation

Three ways to set the sum insured.

The basis you choose decides what a settlement looks like years before a claim is made. Each one answers a different question about the machine.

Agreed Value

A figure fixed in advance

You and the insurer agree the machine's value when the policy is written, and that figure is what responds. It removes the argument at claims stage, but it only holds if the figure was right to begin with.

Market Value

What it is worth today

Settlement reflects what the machine would fetch in its current condition, age and hours. It tracks the market, which also means it falls as the asset ages and may not fund a replacement.

New Replacement

What it costs to replace

Cover set at the cost of equivalent equipment delivered and commissioned at your site. For imported plant that means allowing for exchange rate movement, freight and duties.

Why it's structured separately

Machinery risk moves. Site risk doesn't.

Mobile equipment operates across pits, haul roads and sometimes multiple sites, a different risk pattern to fixed site assets. Reviewing the fleet as its own category means valuations stay current as equipment is added, sold or moved between sites, and claims aren't delayed by disputes over which policy responds.

Is yellow metal covered under the mine's asset policy?

Usually not in full. Fixed plant tends to sit on the asset policy, while mobile equipment is generally rated and insured separately because it moves, operates on uneven ground and carries a different loss profile. Assuming mobile fleet is picked up by the property section is a common source of gaps.

What is the difference between breakdown and all-risk cover?

All-risk responds to sudden external physical damage: fire, impact, overturning. Machinery breakdown responds to internal failure: electrical or mechanical failure, and in many cases operator error. A machine that fails internally with no external damage is a breakdown claim, not a property claim, so a programme carrying only all-risk can leave a large exposure open.

Who insures hired-in or contractors' plant?

It depends on the contract. Many hire agreements pass responsibility for loss or damage to the hirer while the equipment is in their possession, which can leave a mine carrying risk on machines it does not own. We read the hire and contracting terms alongside the policy so that responsibility and cover line up rather than leaving a gap between them.
